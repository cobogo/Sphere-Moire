How it works

A solid glass sphere, cut by a back plane. Pattern A is printed on the curved front, pattern B on the back. Every line of sight pairs a front point P with a back point Q; the spacing of the Q’s compared with the spacing of the P’s is the scale B must have to line up with A. Detune that scale a little and the mismatch becomes a moiré of controlled size.

Line of sight

The ray from the eye meets the front at P, bends toward the surface normal and continues straight through the glass to Q. Ink at P and ink at Q appear on top of each other, so B must carry at Q what A carries at P.

Snell: sin i = n · sin r

A sits on the outer surface and is seen without refraction; only B is seen through the glass.

Why B is smaller

The curved front is a converging lens surface. Near the axis the rays head for a point F′ at s′ behind the front vertex, so at the back, an axial distance L = R + c away, all heights shrink by the same factor m₀.

With the plane at F′, m₀ = 0 and B is hugely magnified; behind F′, B appears inverted.

Away from the axis

Outer rays bend more (spherical aberration), so the scale changes with radius and differs across the rings (mr) and along them (mt). A pre-distorted B follows the exact radial map and matches everywhere, for the design eye. Beyond the fold radius two front points share one back point and nothing can match.

Moiré

Two gratings of pitch p₁ and p₂ at an angle Δθ beat with period

M = p₁p₂ / √(p₁² + p₂² − 2p₁p₂ cos Δθ)

Small detune ε: M ≈ p / ε. Small rotation: M ≈ p / Δθ (radians). Fringes magnify any slip between the layers by M / p.

top: A, middle: B as seen, bottom: both

Pulsing

The scale B needs depends on where the eye is, so with fixed prints the effective detune changes as a visitor moves. Walking toward the sphere changes the scale at every point at once: the fringes open and close, a pulse. Moving sideways makes them slide.

To make the piece open up at a chosen spot, design B for that distance with ε = 0 (lock the design eye there). The “Pulse vs distance” chart shows how many fringes appear across the front at each distance: where it touches zero, the field goes uniform, and on both sides rings come back.

Breathing, swinging and flowing need a back layer that changes or moves: a motorised disc, a display or a projection behind the glass.

Workflow for the two artworks

  1. Set R, n and the back plane c to match your piece, and check the section.
  2. Put the eye at the typical viewing distance, on axis, and tick “Lock design eye”.
  3. Draw A flat with pitch p. It is wrapped onto the front by straight projection along the axis (artwork x, y = surface x, y). If you measure along the curved surface instead, convert with r = R · sin(s / R).
  4. Choose the fringe size M and set ε ≈ p / M, or a rotation Δθ ≈ p / M. For a pulse at a chosen distance, use ε = 0 there.
  5. Make B: scale A by k · (1 + ε) with k = m₀ (simple, good near the centre), or warp it with the radial map below (exact). “The two artworks” shows both at true size. B is drawn as seen from the front; tick “Mirror B” if you print it from behind.
  6. Play “Visitor walks in and out” and “Visitor sways sideways” to check how the effect behaves for real visitors.
