How it works

A solid glass sphere, cut by a back plane. Pattern A is printed on the curved front, pattern B on the back. Every line of sight pairs a front point P with a back point Q, and the spacing of the Q’s compared with the spacing of the P’s is the scale B must have to line up with A. Detune that scale a little and the mismatch becomes a moiré of controlled size.

Line of sight

The ray from the eye meets the front at P, bends toward the surface normal and continues straight through the glass to Q on the back. Ink at P and ink at Q appear on top of each other. For B to line up with A, the back artwork must carry at Q what A carries at P.

Snell: sin i = n · sin r

The front pattern sits on the outer surface, so you see it without refraction. Only B is seen through the glass.

Why B is smaller

The curved front is a converging lens surface. Near the axis the rays inside head for a point F′ at distance s′ behind the front vertex. At the back, an axial distance L = R + c away, every height has shrunk by the same factor m₀.

If the plane sits at F′, m₀ = 0: the whole back collapses into one point and any B pattern is hugely magnified. Behind F′, m₀ is negative and B appears upside down.

Away from the axis

Outer rays hit the surface more steeply and bend more (spherical aberration), so the scale is not constant. It also differs across the rings (mr) and along them (mt). A uniformly scaled B matches only around one radius. A pre-distorted B follows the exact radial map, so it matches everywhere, but strictly only for the design eye.

At some radius the map folds back on itself: two front points see the same back point. Beyond that, no B can match A.

Moiré

Two gratings of pitch p₁ and p₂ at an angle Δθ beat with period

M = p₁p₂ / √(p₁² + p₂² − 2p₁p₂ cos Δθ)

For a small detune ε, M ≈ p / ε; for a small rotation, M ≈ p / Δθ (radians). Fringes magnify any relative slip between the layers by M / p, which is why they sweep across the sphere when you move your head.

Workflow for the two artworks

  1. Set R, n and the back plane c to match your piece, and check the section.
  2. Put the eye at the typical viewing distance, on axis, and tick “Lock design eye”.
  3. Draw pattern A flat with pitch p. It is wrapped onto the front by straight projection along the axis (artwork x, y = surface x, y). If you measure along the curved surface instead, convert with r = R · sin(s / R).
  4. Choose the fringe size M you want and set ε ≈ p / M, or a rotation Δθ ≈ p / M.
  5. Make B: either scale A by k · (1 + ε) with k = m₀ (simple, good near the centre), or warp it with the radial map below (exact). B is given as seen from the front through the glass; mirror it if you print it from behind.
  6. Move α and the distance to check how the fringes drift and how sensitive the result is.
