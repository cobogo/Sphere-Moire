    How it works

    A glass sphere, cut by a back plane. Pattern A is printed on the curved front, pattern B on the back. Every line of sight pairs a front point P with a back point Q; the spacing of the Q’s compared with the spacing of the P’s is the scale B must have to line up with A. Detune that scale a little and the mismatch becomes a moiré of controlled size. The “Two planes” object is the classic set-up for comparison: two flat layers an offset t apart.

    Line of sight

    The ray from the eye meets the front at P, bends toward the surface normal and continues straight to Q. Ink at P and ink at Q appear on top of each other, so B must carry at Q what A carries at P.

    Snell: sin i = n · sin r

    A sits on the outer surface and is seen without refraction; only B is seen through the glass.

    Why B is smaller in the sphere

    The curved front is a converging lens surface. Near the axis the rays head for a point F′ at s′ behind the front, so at the back, an axial distance L = R + c away, all heights shrink by the same factor m₀.

    With the plane at F′, m₀ = 0 and B is hugely magnified; behind F′, B appears inverted.

    Classic two-plane moiré

    With a flat front there is no lens. The back layer is simply farther from the eye, so B must be slightly larger than A, and almost the same scale everywhere:

    Glass between the layers makes B look closer (by t / n), which reduces both the size difference and the parallax.

    Away from the axis

    In the sphere, outer rays bend more (spherical aberration), so the scale changes with radius and differs across the rings (mr) and along them (mt). A pre-distorted B follows the exact radial map and matches everywhere, for the design eye. Beyond the fold radius two front points share one back point and nothing can match.

    Moiré

    Two gratings of pitch p₁ and p₂ at an angle Δθ beat with period

    M = p₁p₂ / √(p₁² + p₂² − 2p₁p₂ cos Δθ)

    Small detune ε: M ≈ p / ε. Small rotation: M ≈ p / Δθ (radians). Fringes magnify any slip between the layers by M / p.

    Designed B: sun spots

    A stays a plain grating. B is the same grating, but shifted inside each Voronoi cell: a random amount per cell, which gives every cell its own tone, plus a shift that grows from the cell centre to its edge, which draws concentric light and dark rings. Seen through A, each cell reads as a glowing spot.

    The cells are defined in A’s coordinates and B is still pre-distorted, so the spots land where you put them on the sphere. As the visitor moves, the parallax adds to every cell’s phase and the spots ripple.

    Pulsing

    The scale B needs depends on where the eye is, so with fixed prints the effective detune changes as a visitor moves. Walking toward the piece makes the fringes open and close, a pulse; moving sideways makes them slide. To make the field open up at a chosen spot, design B for that distance with ε = 0 (lock the design eye there).

    Breathing, swinging and flowing need a back layer that changes or moves: a motorised disc, a display or a projection.

    Workflow for the two artworks

    1. Set R, n and the back plane c to match your piece, and check the section.
    2. Put the eye at the typical viewing distance, on axis, and tick “Lock design eye”.
    3. Draw A flat with pitch p. On the sphere it is wrapped onto the front by straight projection along the axis (artwork x, y = surface x, y). If you measure along the curved surface instead, convert with r = R · sin(s / R).
    4. Choose the fringe size M and set ε ≈ p / M, or a rotation Δθ ≈ p / M. For a pulse at a chosen distance, use ε = 0 there.
    5. Make B: scale A by k · (1 + ε) with k = m₀ (simple, good near the centre), or warp it with the radial map below (exact). B is drawn as seen from the front; tick “Mirror B” if you print it from behind.
    6. Use “Compare” to see how the same artwork behaves in the sphere and on two flat layers, and play the visitor animations.
